Tendon-Driven Robotic Finger

Built the actuation and control system for a backdrivable, four-DOF tendon-driven robotic finger with force control.

Research

[Finger in Custom Motor Mount]

Motivation

Tendon-driven robotic fingers are central to dexterous design. By remotizing force, they can achieve human-like motions in a compact form factor. This is done by utilizing the space available lower in the hand or forearm to store more complex systems and larger motors. This REU project at the NSF Human AugmentatioN via Dexterity Engineering Research Center focused on building a finger that is not only position-controlled but also backdrivable, allowing compliant interaction with objects.

Approach

The finger uses an n+1 underactuated tendon routing scheme with a four-bar coupling on two of its flexion joints, giving it four degrees of freedom, or three independant degree of freedom. The proximal and medial joints form a four-bar linkage, approximated as linear for proximal angles up to ~70°. I was responsible for the full actuation and control pipeline:

  • Built the control system around four ODrive Pro motor controllers driving Maxon ECX Torque XL motors with AS5147 magnetic encoders, communicating over CAN bus via a Teensy 4.1
  • Implemented PID and gravity feedforward control using center-of-mass data from OnShape, with torque balancing and motor torques bounded between 5–25 mNm to manage heating
  • Used motor torques and Jacobians to drive the finger to commanded joint positions
  • Designed and fabricated custom motor mounts in OnShape
  • Managed CAN bus wiring (twisted pairs, 120 Ω terminators, common ground) and debugged signal integrity with an oscilloscope
  • Wired and soldered the full electronics system
  • Built a Python serial UI (Tkinter/Matplotlib) for real-time monitoring, PID tuning, and control

CAN Communication & Calibration

The four ODrive motor controllers communicate over a shared CAN bus, which required careful hardware setup and debugging. CAN relies on twisted-pair differential signaling (CANH/CANL), 120 Ω terminating resistors at each end of the chain, and a common ground across all devices. Issues such as signal noise, bus saturation, and misconfigured node IDs were diagnosed using an oscilloscope connected directly to the CAN lines and a CAN sniffer. I wrote a comprehensive CAN Debugging Guide documenting the troubleshooting process for future users of the system.

Motor calibration was performed through the ODrive GUI by disconnecting the encoder harnesses, removing the pulleys from the motor shafts, running the calibration routine, and then reassembling. Calibrations were saved to each ODrive and needed to be repeated whenever unexpected behavior occurred or a tendon went slack, since slackness indicated the motors were no longer providing the correct torque. On startup, the system collected motor position offsets by driving the finger to a known upright reference position before beginning closed-loop control.

Outcome

The finger demonstrated position tracking and backdrivability across its four degrees of freedom, with the feedforward and PID control scheme successfully driving the finger to commanded joint positions. However, the system faced notable limitations. The linear approximation of the four-bar coupling was only accurate for the upper portion of the proximal joint's range (~70°), and an unresolved error in the joint angle calculation required manual tuning of the feedforward terms.

The ungeared Maxon motors provided high mechanical backdrivability but generated significant heat at the required torques, softening the PLA motor mounts and causing calibrations to drift. The ODrive controllers also introduced intermittent errors and calibration issues that complicated testing. CAN bus saturation with four ODrives sending feedback at 10 ms intervals limited the update frequency, and serial communication for the UI further reduced the control loop from ~200 Hz to ~35 Hz.

Future recommendations include exploring geared motors or higher-temperature mount materials, splitting CAN communication across multiple buses, optimizing the control loop timing, and replacing the linear coupling approximation with a complete function for full-range position control.
